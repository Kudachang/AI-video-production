# Acting task

Adapted from Higgsfield's acting skill (Tigran's directing method, Oct 2026). Makes AI-generated actors read alive by giving the mind behind the face real work, not facial choreography.

## Core principle

An acting task is the character being INVESTED in their tactic for reaching a goal.
- Not external behaviour ("brows lift", "looks sad"). That plays the result and produces dead faces.
- The tactic can live entirely in the eyes: someone arguing from conviction keeps checking both of the partner's eyes for a sparkle of trust and adjusts to what they find.
- Never fix dead eyes with lighting tricks. Fix them by giving the eyes a task.

## The ladder (in order)

0. **Read the whole scene first.** Never build a task from one line.
1. **Scene goal:** one shared, usually unspoken direction all characters play toward (e.g. "part without pain, stay positive"). Not the film's theme; characters never play the film's purposes.
   - **The ending names the event.** Read the scene backward from its last beat; watch for a last line said about one thing and meant about another.
   - **Everyone is in the event,** silent characters included. If someone stands outside it, the event is named wrong.
   - **The physical action is the channel.** The surface activity (packing a suitcase, routine rounds) stays; each character pursues the event through their own visible physical behaviour.
2. **Motive:** each character's own reason for pushing the same direction. Same vector, different fuel. Respect given circumstances.
3. **Goal:** each character's personal fight inside the scene. Never the scene goal's words.
4. **Obstacle:** what presses against the direction; one crack and it collapses. The audience feels this pressure because nobody plays it.
5. **Tactic:** the moment-to-moment pursuit, written as verbs aimed at the partner, with eye-work as purposeful action: checking both eyes for trust; registering whether a point landed; stealing looks and snapping back before being caught; measuring and memorising the partner.

## Prompt block format

```
ACTING TASK — [NAME] (fully invested in her tactic; the work happens in her eyes):
SCENE DIRECTION (shared, unspoken): [one line]
MOTIVE: [why she pushes that direction]
GOAL: [her personal fight]
OBSTACLE: [what presses against the line]
TACTIC: [what she does to the partner, with the eye-work as action]
Moment to moment:
— "[dialogue words]" — [verb at the partner + what the eyes check]
— [where the line breaks, if it breaks]
(Gaze always engaged in the task, never a frozen or glassy stare; natural blink cadence.)
```

Rules:
- Verbs aimed at the partner. No emotion adjectives as instruction ("sadly", "nervously").
- No facial choreography beyond the one safety line.
- Nobody plays the emotion; everyone plays the direction.
- Silent listeners get a real task too ("decide if she's serious", "protect the mood").

## Contrast pairs

In a two-character scene, give each a plus and a minus, mirrored against the other, and name the essential axis the audience reads. Both still push the same scene direction; the contrast leaks through the tactics and the eyes. A trait that seems like one thing is often scar tissue over its opposite.

## Worked example

Mother packs her son's suitcase an hour before he leaves for the army. Direction: part without pain. Her motive: tears before a journey are a bad omen. His: for her. Tactic: she packs "ordinarily", eyes stealing to him, memorising him, snapping back to the socks when he looks up; he eats "casually", watching her hands, not her face, because her face is where the danger is. The audience cries because nobody on screen does.

## Common failures

- Task built from one line: re-read the whole scene.
- The film's theme assigned as a character's task.
- Scene goal confused with character goal.
- Motive that ignores given circumstances.
- Face choreography instead of eye-work as action.
- Emotion adjectives as direction.
